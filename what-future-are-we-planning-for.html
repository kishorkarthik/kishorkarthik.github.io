Who Are We Becoming Better For?

June 2026



Most of us have imagined a different version of ourselves one or five years from now—a life that somehow feels more complete than the one we are living today. We invest months, sometimes years, planning and working toward that future. Then, one day we look back and realize that despite all that effort, much of what feels different came from time passing rather than from the plan itself.

It is an uncomfortable realization: two years into a plan, we find ourselves pretty much where we started. This cycle repeats over and over. So, where does planning go wrong?

To understand that, we have to look at what planning actually is. In fact, planning is something everybody does all the time. For instance, if you are driving to work, you are likely to decide whether or not you need gasoline. You look at the fuel gauge to see how much gas you have in the tank, estimate how far you need to go, and make a rough guess as to how much gas you need to complete your drive. By comparing in your mind the gas you need with the gas you have, you decide whether you should stop for gas or not.

Abstracting from this example, planning follows a simple three-step process:


Step 1: Establish projected need or demand.

Step 2: Establish your present status.

Step 3: Compare and reconcile steps 1 and 2.


Put another way: what more (or less) do you need to do to close the gap between your projected need or demand and your present status?

This process is remarkably effective because it naturally leads to action. Once we know what we need, where we are, and what separates the two, the next step becomes obvious. Whether we're planning a journey, managing a project, or preparing for something bigger, reducing uncertainty into a concrete gap makes progress measurable.

The difficulty begins when we apply the same process to the distant future. Unlike planning a journey to work, we often overlook the very questions that make planning effective. Rather than carefully defining what we need, where we are today, and what separates the two, we jump straight to the destinations we hope to reach.

The farther into the future we project, the more likely our understanding of what matters is to change. Instead of following a perfectly predetermined route, it's better to adjust our course as the journey reveals new information. Rather than trying to determine every step years in advance, it is better to begin with something we can do today—something achievable in the near term, but capable of fundamentally changing the trajectory thereafter.

Perhaps the answer is not to plan for the future all at once, but to plan for the next point at which the future becomes a little clearer than it is today: for the nearby inflection points.

But what exactly is an inflection point? An inflection point is a pivotal moment or turning point where a significant and lasting change takes place.

Let me share a personal example. When I was in eight grade, I scored 66.4% in an examination and finished eighth in my class. Feeling disappointed, I felt I was capable of more, so I prepared differently for the next examination. When the results came out, I had scored 84% and finished second.

At first glance, this seems like nothing more than a story about improving exam scores.

I don't think it is.

The result changed more than my marks; it changed my starting point. Every future decision was now being made from a different position than before. The examination changed the assumptions behind my plan. Before it, I planned as someone trying to prove I was capable of doing well. After it, I planned as someone who knew they were capable.

What changed was my understanding of where I stood—and, with it, the future I could reasonably plan for. Before the examination, the answers to the three planning questions were one thing. After the examination, they became something else. My present status had changed. The gap I was trying to close had changed. Even what I needed next had changed. The same planning process still applied, but it was now solving a different problem. That is why the examination became an inflection point.

The future doesn't arrive all at once. Neither should our plans. It unfolds through nearby inflection points—moments that change where we stand, what we need, and what we do next.

Ultimately, we must ask ourselves: what future are we planning for? The future for which we can meaningfully answer the three planning questions. Every nearby inflection point gives us better answers, and those better answers become the foundation of the next plan.

Why Do Things Tend to Get Worse If Left Alone?

August 2026

As children, we rarely question the things that quietly hold our lives together. We assume the people around us will always be there. Home feels permanent. Friendships feel effortless. We never stop to wonder whether any of it could slowly change. We simply live, believing tomorrow will look much like today.

As we grow older, we begin to notice what we never had to think about before. The people around us become busier. Time together becomes less frequent. The things that once felt permanent begin to feel more fragile. Not because they disappear all at once, but because they change so gradually that we hardly notice until we look back.

It made me wonder whether this was simply a part of growing older, or whether I was noticing something deeper. The more I looked around, the more I found the same pattern beyond human life. A house left unattended gathers dust. A garden left untended becomes overgrown. A bicycle left in the rain slowly rusts. A codebase left untouched slowly accumulates technical debt. Different things, different causes, yet they all seemed to have one thing in common—they tended not to remain as they were.

I couldn't help but wonder: why? Why did so many different things, governed by different rules and shaped by different forces, seem to share the same tendency?

Why do things tend to get worse if left alone? Good questions often begin with patterns. When different phenomena point in the same direction, we begin to wonder whether they share a deeper explanation. This seemed to be one of those questions.

The question I was asking has been explored by scientists for centuries. One of the most fundamental ideas in physics, the Second Law of Thermodynamics, describes a similar tendency: isolated systems naturally move toward greater entropy over time. While this principle governs the behavior of physical systems, its underlying idea feels surprisingly familiar. Things rarely remain the way we leave them.

Entropy is often described as a measure of disorder. In simple terms, systems naturally tend to move from more organized states toward states with greater disorder over time. This does not happen because something is actively trying to break them apart, but because there are far more ways for a system to exist in a disordered state than an ordered one. Maintaining order requires energy.

It does not say that things are doomed to decay. It says that preserving a state requires energy.

Imagine sitting around a campfire with the people you love. The warmth reaches everyone equally. The flickering flames push back the darkness, making the night feel a little safer, a little more welcoming. Conversations seem to last longer. Laughter comes more easily. For a moment, it feels as though the fire could burn forever. Yet no one truly believes it will. Every now and then, someone quietly leans forward and places another log onto the flames. It is such an ordinary act that it almost goes unnoticed. But without it, the fire would slowly begin to fade, the warmth would disappear. Before long, all that remains is a bed of ash.

Perhaps we aren't so different. Left alone for long enough, something within us begins to change. Not dramatically, but quietly. Conversations become less frequent. Laughter becomes less familiar. The feeling that someone is there—that the world is steady and welcoming—slowly gives way to uncertainty. We do not lose ourselves overnight. We simply begin to feel the absence of what once quietly held us together. That may be because we were never meant to hold ourselves together entirely on our own.

We often mistake stability for permanence because we rarely see the work behind it. It is only when things begin to change that we realize how fragile they have always been.

The lesson isn't that everything is destined to fall apart. Perhaps it's that the things we cherish most were never holding themselves together in the first place. Like a campfire on a cold night, they have always depended on someone quietly placing another log onto the flames.
